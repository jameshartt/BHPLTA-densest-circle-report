# Headline / tone review

*Review of the Step 7 Headline blockquote (lines ~753–803) in
`reports/densest_circle_full_writeup.md`, judged against the evidence in
Steps 0–7 and the audit confidence ratings.*

## What works

- **The numbers are anchored, not loose.** "43 courts" / "10.9 km² of
  land" / "3.94 per km²" are all reproducible and traceable, and the
  blockquote lists the seven named venues — a journalist can spot-check
  every figure within 5 minutes.
- **The competitor list is concrete.** Naming "Manhattan-bordering
  NYC", "the Australian Open's Melbourne", "the Tennis Paris municipal
  system", "Hyde Park Chicago", "Boston's DCR Charles River
  Reservation" forces the comparison to be visualised rather than
  abstract. Strong press-release instinct.
- **The Tokyo asterisk is handled in plain English** inside the
  headline itself, not buried in a footnote — that's the right call
  for press-release context where a journalist will absolutely ask
  "but what about Tokyo?"
- **The 36%-in-the-Channel framing is a gift** — it makes the
  "small-with-a-disadvantage" angle land before the reader even gets
  to population.

## What's missing or under-sold

- **The smallness vs giants story is buried.** It only arrives in
  paragraph five of the blockquote, after a 7-row ranking table and a
  Tokyo digression. The single most newsworthy fact — Brighton 280k
  beating Paris/London/NYC — should be the opening line.
- **No human-scale anchor.** "2.34 km circle" is technical. The
  writeup contains the perfect phrase ("25 minutes to walk corner-to-
  corner", line 89) but the headline doesn't reuse it. A reader who
  doesn't live in Brighton has no intuition for what 2.34 km feels like.
- **The "fair denominator" rule is not explained in the headline at
  all.** A tennis fan reading only the blockquote sees Paris drop from
  4.48 to 2.56 with a one-line parenthetical about "Val-de-Marne LAND".
  That is the load-bearing methodology move of the whole writeup, and
  it gets one clause. A non-technical reader will smell sleight of hand.
- **No anticipatory rebuttal of the "but Wimbledon!" reflex.** Every
  British reader will immediately think "surely SW19?". The writeup
  addresses this (Step 1: Wimbledon's cluster is mostly the All England
  LTC, which you can't book), but the headline doesn't pre-empt it.
- **The "Brighton in top 8 under 1M population" stat is the killer
  fact** but it's currently the *fifth* point in the blockquote. It
  should be in the lede.

## What could attract pushback (and how to pre-empt it)

- **"Densest cluster of public, accessible park-tennis courts of any
  major city in the world"** is doing a lot of work. A Parisian
  journalist could legitimately argue that Paris has *more* publicly-
  bookable courts (43 vs 43 tied numerator, and 16 more in the
  wider 75 admin if you include Tennis Paris's full network). The
  honest framing is "highest *density* per km² of reachable land",
  not "the most" or "the best". The current headline phrasing
  blurs the two.
- **The Step 7 reranking is the right call but reads as a rule
  changed at the last minute** to land Brighton at #1. The fact that
  Boston has been flagged as a denominator artefact since Step 4 is
  the strongest defence — the headline should mention this lineage
  explicitly ("the same rule we applied to Boston since Step 4").
- **Audit confidence is "high" for Brighton, "medium" for Paris,
  Melbourne, NYC, etc.** A skeptical reader can argue: Brighton has
  been audited venue-by-venue, every other city by a single agent's
  web search. That cuts both ways — it is Brighton's strongest defence
  *and* a methodological asymmetry a Telegraph fact-checker will
  flag. Better to own it in the headline than have it surfaced
  against you.
- **"Publicly-bookable" vs "free to enter".** BHPLTA courts mostly
  cost money to book; so do Tennis Paris's. NYC's are largely free
  with a permit. The categories are not perfectly aligned and a sharp
  journalist will notice. The Terminology note handles this — the
  headline doesn't.
- **The Hove Beach Park manual add (+6 courts).** A pedantic auditor
  will note that Brighton's ground-truth correction adds courts that
  *aren't* OSM-tagged as being in a park; if other cities have the
  same untagged-but-real gap (the writeup explicitly says they
  almost certainly do, line 441), Brighton's #1 is contingent on
  Brighton having been audited harder than anyone else. Step 6 owns
  this. The headline should too — one sentence.

## Three specific recommended edits

1. **Replace the opening sentence with a smallness-vs-giants lede.**
   Something like: *"Brighton & Hove — a coastal city of 280,000,
   with more than a third of its central circle sitting in the
   English Channel — has a denser cluster of publicly-bookable
   park-tennis courts within a 25-minute walk than Paris, New York,
   Melbourne, Manhattan or central London."* That is the
   press-release sentence. Put the 3.94 / km² number second.
2. **Add a 30-word "why this isn't sleight of hand" sentence** right
   after the ranking table. Suggested wording: *"The single
   methodology change between Step 6 (Brighton #3) and Step 7
   (Brighton #1) — counting only water, not neighbouring-city land,
   as 'unreachable' — is the same fix already flagged for Boston in
   Step 4, applied consistently to all 16 cities."* This pre-empts the
   "you changed the rule" rebuttal.
3. **Anchor the cluster in a named Brighton landmark** rather than
   "Brunswick / Norfolk Square" coordinates. The current opening uses
   the three anchor parks (Queens / Kingsway / Blakers) but doesn't
   say *where the centre is* in a way Brightonians would recognise.
   "Centred on the seafront between Brunswick Square and the i360"
   (or similar — pick a landmark that's actually near 50.8257°N,
   0.1565°W) gives a reader a mental pin to drop. Pair it with the
   "25-minute walk corner-to-corner" phrase.

## Verdict

Not quite press-release-ready, but two-thirds of the way there. The
evidence supports the #1 claim under the fair-denominator rule, and
the writeup is impressively honest about *which* rule is being
applied. The biggest single fix is rewriting the lede: the
smallness-vs-giants story is the news, and it's currently the fifth
paragraph instead of the first. The second-biggest is one explicit
sentence defending the Step-7 rule change against the
"changed-the-rule-to-win" rebuttal — without it, an unfriendly
journalist has an easy "well actually" angle.
